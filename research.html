Working Papers

Conflict Exposure and Son Preference: Evidence from Nepal's Civil War

Click here for the most recent version

Son preference has been shown to drive sex-selective abortion and excess female mortality in much of Asia. Literature links these outcomes to the spread of ultrasound combined with factors like fertility decline and socio-economic changes. Armed conflict has received far less attention, and none has asked whether conflict experienced in adolescence, when attitudes form, changes son preference. I examine whether exposure to Nepal's 1996–2006 armed conflict during adolescent years increased women's revealed preference for sons, comparing women who were adolescents during the conflict with older women across more and less affected districts. Women raised in high-conflict districts had fewer daughters and were more likely to stop childbearing after a son: approximately one fewer daughter per eight women between lightly and heavily affected districts. The sex of the next child and birth spacing after daughters are unchanged, indicating that son-conditional stopping, rather than sex-selective abortion, is the likely channel. Broader gender attitudes and life outcomes show little or no change.
